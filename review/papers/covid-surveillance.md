# Community surveillance and outdoor contact rule

## 1. Decision and proposal
Decision date: 15 May 2020. Jurisdiction: a national health authority. Population: residents aged two and over living in private households; institutions remain under separate rules. The committee is considering replacing the prohibition on meeting another household outdoors with permission to meet one person from another household, maintaining two metres' separation, from 18 May for a 14-day trial. Indoor meetings, organized gatherings and institutional visiting remain prohibited. Alternatives are retaining the current rule for seven days pending another surveillance round, or allowing the same outdoor contact only in two districts with delivery capacity. No option includes unrestricted gatherings.

Recorded positive results fell by 40% between the two reporting weeks. The proposal combines this signal with the lower contact intensity of the specified activity; it is not a proposal to remove all restrictions. The evidence below concerns infection trends before the proposed change, not its effect after introduction.

## 2. Objectives and decision standard
The authority's health committee prioritizes avoiding preventable severe illness and preserving hospital capacity. Its resident advisory panel prioritizes reducing isolation and allowing low-cost contact, including for people without private gardens. Neither group has assigned a monetary value to illness or established how to trade every health risk against isolation.

The planning horizon is 14 days, with review after seven days. The committee's acceptable-risk rule permits a reversible trial when no supplied evidence indicates an ongoing increase in community incidence and safeguards can be delivered. This is a decision rule under uncertainty, not a guarantee of no additional infections. A firmer downward trend strengthens the case but is not an absolute prerequisite. Members must state how they weigh residual uncertainty against another week of isolation.

## 3. Evidence available by 14 May
The reporting weeks are 20–26 April and 4–10 May. Routine diagnostic records contain 100 positive results among 20,000 people tested in the first week and 60 among 12,000 in the second. People are counted once per week. Eligibility moved from a wider occupational and symptom referral list to a narrower urgent-referral list as mobile collection teams were reassigned. Diagnostic capacity, referral composition and uptake therefore changed between weeks.

Neither series measures institutions. Newly positive household swabs are a proxy for interval infections, not a direct count of infectious contacts or a measurement of the effect of permitting outdoor meetings. No district-specific estimates precise enough for separate trend judgments are supplied.

## 4. Delivery, resources and consequences
A £200,000 contingency allocation covers the next surveillance round, public communications and a seven-day review; four mobile teams can maintain the existing surveillance schedule. Results take three days after collection. More frequent population-wide swabbing is not funded. Two districts can implement a geographically limited trial, but travel across boundaries would complicate communication and enforcement.

Acting can create additional contacts, unequal opportunities to comply and confusion about indoor rules. Waiting prolongs isolation and restricts residents' access to social support; the advisory panel particularly identifies people living alone. Numerical estimates of these effects are not supplied. Communications must distinguish outdoor permission from indoor permission and provide accessible advice. Hospital admissions will be reviewed daily, but can lag infections. A sustained rise across two surveillance rounds or a hospital-capacity alert triggers urgent reconsideration; reversal requires a new public notice within 48 hours.

## 5. Exact claim for assessment
“Community infection incidence in private-household residents was lower in 4–10 May than in 20–26 April, rather than the recorded decline arising solely from altered access to routine diagnostic testing.”

Assess this claim separately from whether the proposed outdoor-contact trial is proportionate.

## Version X

### Passage 1

A separate household surveillance programme used independently drawn probability samples of private-household residents for each week. Each participant had a negative swab at the start and a scheduled swab seven days later, regardless of symptoms or use of diagnostic services. In each week, 25,000 residents were invited and 20,000 completed both swabs. New positive results were 100/20,000 and 60/20,000, respectively: 0.50% and 0.30%. The estimated second-to-first positive-result ratio was 0.60, with a household-cluster-adjusted 95% interval of 0.42–0.86. Age, region and household-size weights were fixed before analysis; weighted and unweighted ratios agreed to two decimal places. Participation by these strata was stable. Sampling, swab procedures, laboratory thresholds and turnaround were unchanged. The analysis assumes stable detection of infections across weeks and comparable participation within weighting strata; brief infections between visits can escape detection.

### Passage 1

A separate household surveillance programme used independently drawn probability samples of private-household residents for each week. Each participant had a negative swab at the start; a second swab during the next seven days was arranged through routine diagnostic services following referral, rather than a scheduled visit. In each week, 25,000 residents were invited and 20,000 completed the initial swab. New positive results were 100/20,000 and 60/20,000, respectively: 0.50% and 0.30%. The estimated second-to-first positive-result ratio was 0.60, with a household-cluster-adjusted 95% interval of 0.42–0.86. Age, region and household-size weights were fixed before analysis; weighted and unweighted ratios agreed to two decimal places. Participation by these strata was stable. Initial sampling, swab procedures, laboratory thresholds and turnaround were unchanged. Follow-up swabs were completed by 2,000 residents in the first week and 1,200 in the second, under the diagnostic referral rules described above. Residents without a follow-up positive result remained in the initial-sample denominator. The analysis assumes stable detection of infections across weeks and comparable participation within weighting strata; brief infections between visits can escape detection.
